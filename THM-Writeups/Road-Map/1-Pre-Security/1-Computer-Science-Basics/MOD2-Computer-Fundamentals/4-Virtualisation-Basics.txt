Introduction:
	. Learning Objectives:
		. Understand why managing applications on individual physical servers is inefficient
		. Learn how virtualization addresses hardware utilization and scalability challenges
		. Understand the components of a lab machine
		. Learn how containers have further optimized hardware utilization for applications
		
Virtualization Overview:
	. Before virtualization, the rule of thumb in IT was: one server = one application
	. In the early days of IT, digital services were run exclusively on physical hardware
		. Each physical machine would usually have a clear purpose (hosting a website, storing data, etc.)
	. Problems with the "One job per box" approach:
		. High Cost - Buying multiple physical servers is expensive, not just the hardware itself, but the electricity, space, and maintenance
		. Low Utilization - Most applications don't use the server's full capacity (most servers stayed around 5-20% utilization), wasting CPU, memory, and storage resources
		. Slow Deployment - Setting up new physical servers could take days or weeks
		. Hard to Scale - If an application suddenly needed more resources, you would have to buy yet another server
		. In short, companies were paying a lot for hardware that wasn’t being fully utilized.
	. The Need for Sharing Hardware Safely and Efficiently
		. Virtualization introduced a new idea: What if multiple applications could share the same physical hardware safely?
		. A virtualization layer, called a hypervisor, was introduced to act as a referee between lab machines
			. Allows each virtual computer to behave independently, like a physical computer
	. The Building Analogy
		. Imagine one person lives alone in an entire 10-floor building:
			. The person uses 1 floor but has to maintain the other 9 (electricity, cleaning, etc.)
			. Most of the building is unused
			. It's expensive, inefficient, and unnecessary for the person's needs
		. Now imagine dividing the building into separate apartments
			. Each apartment has its own privacy
			. Different people can live independently without bothering each other
			. They all share the building's main structure, which makes it cheaper and more efficient for everyone
		. This is virtualization:
			. The building - The physical hardware
			. Each apartment - Each lab machine in your hypervisor
			. The tenants - applications/operating systems
			. The building manager - The hypervisor (software that divides each machine safely)
	. Each VM acts as an independent system with its own OS, applications, and settings
	. Virtualization enables multiple applications to share the same physical server
	
Virtualization Components
	. Hypervisor (The building manager)
		. This is the core technology behind virtualization
		. It's the software that:
			. Divides a physical computer into multiple virtual computers
			. Gives each lab machine its own share of the host computer's hardware (CPU, storage, and RAM)
			. Keeps everything isolated from VM to VM
			. Manages the life cycle of each VM (start, stop, pause, delete)
		. Hypervisor Implementation Types:
			. Type 1 hypervisor: Runs directly on the physical hardware
				. Fast, efficient, and ideal for servers and professional environments
			. Type 2 hypervisor: Runs within the existing OS
				. Easy to install, ideal for learning / running small setups
		. Hypervisor Type Use Cases:
			. Type 1:
				. Production server
				. Database server
				. Data center
			. Type 2:
				. Test malicious files
				. Software testing
				. Kali Linux
	. Lab Machines (the apartments)
		. A Lab Machine (VM) is a virtual computer created by the hypervisor
			. Behaves as a real machine although it is virtual
		. A virtual machine:
			. Has its own virtual hardware (CPU, RAM, storage, and network)
			. Can run any OS (Windows, Linux, Mac, etc.)
			. Is completely isolated from other VMs in the hypervisor
				. If VM-1 breaks, VM-2 and VM-3 will be unaffected
		. VMs can be deployed using hypervisors like VirtualBox or VMware (these are type 2 examples)
		. Why use a VM?
			. If you want to get some practice with another OS
			. If you want to test malicious files
	. Containers (the rooms inside each apartment)
		. A lightweight, isolated environment that runs a single application (and all necessary app components)
			. Instead of bringing an entire separate OS
		. A container borrows the core of the existing system by running in the kernel
			. Kernel - the part of the OS that communicates with the hardware and manages resources (e.g. memory, programs, etc.)
		. Because containers share the kernel, they start up fast and use less resources
			. This means you must match the host system's type (you cannot run a Windows container in Linux)
		. Containers behave like small, self-contained spaces because:
			. They package the application and its dependencies (tools, versions, etc.)
			. They share the host's operating system (this means they start instantly)
			. They remain isolated from each other (a misbehaving container doesn't affect the others)
			. They can run consistently on any machine (perfect for development/testing and scalable deployments)
		. The easiest way to deploy a container inside a VM is through Docker
			. Docker is an open-source software platform that simplifies the process of building and deploying containers
